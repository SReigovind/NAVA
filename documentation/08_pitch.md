# NAVA — Project Pitch

## What if every farmer had a plant doctor in their pocket?

---

When Priya's banana crop started showing dark spots on the leaves, she did what most smallholder farmers in Kerala do. She waited. She asked a neighbour. She applied a solution she'd heard about from someone two seasons ago, uncertain whether it was the right fungicide or the wrong one. By the time a real expert could look at it, the infection had already spread to half the plants.

Priya's story isn't unusual. It plays out across millions of farms, every season, in every country where access to agricultural expertise is scarce — which is most of them. The world loses an estimated **20–30% of crop yields annually** to plant diseases alone. Not because we don't know how to fight them. But because the knowledge doesn't reach the farmer in time.

**NAVA was built to change that.**

---

## What NAVA actually does

NAVA is not just a disease detector. It's a complete digital agronomist that lives in the browser.

Point your phone at a leaf. Upload the photo. Within seconds, NAVA tells you what's wrong — not with a vague confidence percentage, but with a visual explanation that shows *exactly where on the leaf* the model found the disease signature. That heatmap, generated by a technique called Grad-CAM, is the difference between "the AI said so" and "look at this region — that's where the infection is concentrated." It makes the AI explainable, not just accurate.

But NAVA doesn't stop at diagnosis. It watches. Every time you upload a photo of the same plant, it analyses the near-infrared reflectance of the leaf tissue — a physiological signal that changes before visible symptoms appear. If your plant is beginning to experience stress, NAVA will tell you that *today*, not in two weeks when the spots become visible. That's the difference between prevention and damage control.

And then NAVA talks to you. Ask it anything about the crop. "Should I apply fungicide now?" "What are the recommended varieties for laterite soil?" "How do I manage the previous disease without hurting the soil?" NAVA draws on verified agricultural extension documents to answer — not the internet's general knowledge, but the Kerala Agricultural University's Package of Practices, disease management guides, field-tested advice. It tells you where the information comes from. It doesn't make things up.

It also remembers. Across every conversation, across the entire growing season, NAVA builds a rolling memory of your farm's history. It knows what happened to Plant-1 last month. It knows what treatments you tried. It knows what your soil type is. Every answer it gives is grounded in *your* specific context — not a generic crop database, but your actual farm.

## The design choices that made NAVA different

Building an AI tool for agriculture is easy. Building one that farmers can actually trust is much harder.

**We started with the failure modes of existing tools.**

Most agricultural AI systems are trained on clean, lab-quality images from controlled environments. They break the moment you photograph a leaf in a rice paddy at noon with imperfect lighting and a cluttered background. We built our training dataset differently — aggregating six different open-source repositories, imposing strict class balance rules (no class with fewer than 300 samples, none allowed to dominate above 700), and applying aggressive augmentation to simulate real field conditions. The result: 94.54% accuracy on a hold-out test set. Across 34 disease categories and 7 crops.

**We took explainability seriously.**

When NAVA says "this is Late Blight," it also shows you the Grad-CAM attention map — a coloured overlay on your photo that highlights the exact pixels the model relied on. This isn't cosmetic. It builds trust. If the heatmap highlights a healthy region of the leaf, the farmer should be skeptical. If it pinpoints an obvious lesion, the diagnosis is validated visually without any technical knowledge.

**We designed the confidence system to protect, not impress.**

When the model is not confident enough (below 80%), NAVA flags the result as unreliable — in clear language, not in percentages. It doesn't try to impress the user with a bold diagnosis. It tells them to treat the result with caution, and to consult a human expert. A wrong confident diagnosis is far more dangerous than an honest uncertain one.

**We eliminated hallucinated advice.**

This was the hardest problem. Large language models are conversational and fluent, but they sometimes state incorrect information with complete confidence. For a farmer deciding how much fungicide to apply, "sometimes" is not acceptable. Our solution: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). NAVA's chat system retrieves relevant passages from verified agricultural documents before answering questions that require factual knowledge. The LLM is told to use this material as authoritative ground truth. The sources are shown directly in the chat interface. It's not a black box — you can see exactly where the advice came from.

**We built memory into the system.**

Most chatbots have no memory. Every conversation starts fresh. NAVA maintains a rolling summary of every session, compressing old conversations into memory that persists across weeks and months. When you ask "how is Plant-1 doing compared to last month?" NAVA knows the answer — because it kept notes.

## What was actually built

We built this from scratch over the course of two years as an M.Sc. thesis project at Mahatma Gandhi University's School of Artificial Intelligence and Robotics.

**Phase 1** established the core diagnostic pipeline. We ran a formal model comparison study (ResNet-50 vs. MobileNetV2 vs. EfficientNet-B0), constructed the training dataset, trained and evaluated the disease classifier, and validated the RAG pipeline's effectiveness at preventing hallucinated agricultural advice.

**Phase 2** — the version you're looking at — is the full-stack deployment. Everything a farmer would actually need: user accounts, multi-field farm management, plant-level tracking, scan history, a chat interface that talks about your specific farm, early stress detection before visible symptoms form, and a mobile-responsive web app that works on a smartphone.

The backend runs on a single Python process — no cloud dependency, no external database, no DevOps complexity. The ML models run on CPU. The vector knowledge base stores agricultural extension documents locally. The entire system can be deployed on a modest server (or even a Raspberry Pi) and used in areas with limited connectivity.

**Total scope:**
- **34 disease classes** across 7 crops (rice, banana, tomato, corn, soybean, cassava, cucumber)
- **94.54% disease classification accuracy** on held-out test data
- **28 dB PSNR / 0.85 SSIM** for the VNIR estimation model
- **Hybrid RAG system** with semantic + keyword retrieval and LLM-based reranking
- **Full-stack web application** (FastAPI + React 18 + ChromaDB + SQLite + ONNX)
- **Multilingual by design** — the LLM layer handles queries in any language

## The deeper problem we were solving

Access to agricultural expertise is a problem of distribution, not knowledge. The knowledge exists — in universities, in extension services, in decades of published research. What doesn't exist is a reliable, affordable, always-available channel from that knowledge to the farmers who need it most.

NAVA is a step toward that channel. It's not perfect. No AI system is. But it's a system that knows when to be humble — that flags low-confidence predictions, shows its sources, warns when it's uncertain, and urges the farmer to consult a real expert for high-stakes decisions.

That epistemic humility, we believe, is what makes it potentially trustworthy at scale.
